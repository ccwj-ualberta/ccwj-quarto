Project: Title: Closed-Loop Microcontroller Torch Control In Welding Automation.
An automated vertical standoff system for a stationary GMAW torch will be designed in this project. This project is intended to improve the weld quality by regulating the current in gas metal arc welding process (GMAW) for this purpose an electro-mechanical system consisting of a linear guide rail and an electric actuator carrying the welding torch should modify the contact tip to workpiece distance (CTWD). The prototype uses a microcontroller in conjunction with sensors to acquire, analyze and control the vertical position of the welding torch in response to geometric changes in the surface of the welding plate that are sensed through the fluctuations in the welding current.
In GMAW, voltage and wire-feed speed are controlled and kept constant. An important factor that translates into a quality weld is to keep the CTWD constant, however due to imperfections in the material’s surface the CTWD changes translating into a change in current due to a direct relationship, creating discrepancies and thus bad quality in the weld. For this purpose, the automated CTWD-Torch control is being developed using a feedback control loop to tackle this perturbances and maintain a set point according to the client’s needs.

Project: Quantification of phase transformations using calorimetry and dilatometry
Dilatometry and calorimetry are well-established techniques, and have been used successfully for decades; however, they are seldom used to quantify the progress of a transformation. Most often, these techniques are used to detect start and finish of transformations. When used quantitatively, current analysis of dilation data does not account for the different changes in density for the multiple transformed phases. Similarly, quantitative calorimetric analysis does not account for different rates of enthalpy release for different transformed phases. The technique proposed for both dilatometry and calorimetry consists on posing a differential equation based on dilation or temperature data generated under controlled experimental conditions. When integrated, this equation extracts phase fraction evolution from the experimental data. Like all differential equations, the equation posed involves coefficients and integration constants. The work presented differs from other similar work in that the coefficients are obtained from calibration before, after, and at transition points for each transformation, with a minimum of need of previously tabulated data. These methods can go beyond any previous approach by quantifying partial transformations and making in-situ measurements of phase fractions in complex simultaneous phase transformations possible. This is possible because of a rigorous framework that reduces the number of unknown parameters to its minimum. The mathematical treatments will be introduced, and applications will be discussed involving precipitation during solidification in aluminum A356 alloy, martensitic transformation in creep-resistant steel, and simultaneous bainitic and martensitic transformations in AISI 4140 steel.

Project: Microstructural Characterization of Chromium Carbide Overlays and a Study of Alternative Welding Processes for Industrial Wear Applications
A main outcome of the research was a better understanding of chromium carbide overlay (CCO) microstructures and how they are formed. Overlays such as CCOs overlays are used to extend the service life of a component by adding a wear-resistant layer to of the surface of a cheaper and tougher base material. These overlays are commonly used in all sorts of industries including agriculture, mining, and Oil and Gas. Understanding the microstructural characteristics of CCOs will help develop products that have better and more uniform wear resistance throughout. One key finding of this research was that as the balance was decreased in SAW the amount of dilution declined, which led to higher amounts of hypereutectic microstructure, higher volume fraction of primary carbides, and larger primary carbide size.

Project: Coupling modeling of heat transfer and plastic deformation of Friction Stir Welding
The idea behind this project is to understand one of the newer welding processes, the friction stir welding (FSW) process. Given that despite being highly relevant to aerospace and other state-of-the-art manufacturing industries, FSW is currently unable to use engineering tools such as formulas or tabulated data.
This thesis will focus on modeling coupled heat transfer and plastic deformation in metals when welded, with a focus on providing a general quantitative set of formulas and tabulated data useful in both research and industry. Seeking to validate the generalization of these formulas and increase the currently existing database by contrasting the results with the literature.
This will generalize current data that is currently not connected or has been of little use in industry and research. In this project, the student will learn the theory behind FSW and become familiar with the fundamental and current literature. It will have direct access to the FSW machine, data acquisition systems and metallographic analysis equipment from the University of Alberta Welding Laboratory. The use of programming and data processing software such as Matlab, Python, etc. is expected.
The scope of the project will be that the process will be considered in a general way, subject to industrial implementation in the form of formulas, tables and graphs for simple use for engineers.

Project: Detection of weld defect using deep learning method
The project you will carry out focuses on applied analysis of electrical signals in welding using deep learning for the identification of weld discontinuities. The machine learning approach is based on the Convolution Neural Network (CNN) and deep learning techniques, which have attracted a lot of attention for automated identification of images and regression. We are working on applying the same technique on electrical signals obtained during the weld. Currently, industry relies on external technology such as weld imaging and non-destructive testing (NDT) to identify defects. These techniques are limited in their in-line ability to detect discontinuities inside a weld, and require an expert. Especially for critical welds this results in a slow process. The project aims to open the possibility of automated survey of an existing database of weld signals and builds on work currently carried out at the CCWJ. This project is setting the basis for a “big data” approach to research. Project: Comparative study of angular distortion in welded joints
Welding is a widely used area in the industry, showing itself as an effective and low-cost solution when permanently joining two metal parts, being used to manufacture and repair tanks to pipelines.
One of the problems presented during the process is caused by the dimensional changes produced by the technique, causing deformation in the piece and cracking in the weld bead due to the concentration of efforts in the area, which ends up affecting the final piece reducing its reliability, these changes are known as distortions.
Within the spectrum of existing distortions, the angular distortion for the study to be carried out is chosen, since it is within the most commonly presented distortions, we will begin by investigating the subject by consulting the existing bibliography, and then defining the type of union to analyze depending on the possibilities offered by the experimental method using arc welding as a resource. To compare the experimental results, a numerical model will be generated using a FEM software. Once the data from both tests have been obtained, the results will be concluded.
